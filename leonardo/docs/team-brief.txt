RehabTrack Stroke — Team Brief
Oct 23, 2026 · @Raghav Gupta
TL;DR
Pitch: Stroke recovery depends on daily repetition at home, but the care team can't see
what happens there. RehabTrack measures the weak arm against the healthy arm every
session with a camera, tracks daily arm use and heart health with an Apple Watch, and
gives the therapist what they need to adjust the plan between visits.
Scope (say this out loud to judges): adults with one-sided arm and hand weakness after
a stroke, doing seated exercises at home. Not legs, speech, walking or memory .
What we build:
1. Patient web app — guided exercise sessions with live camera feedback, today's plan,
check-in countdown.
2. Care-team dashboard — progress trends, alerts, 3D replay of sessions, one-click
approval of the next plan.
3. Apple Watch data pipeline — health data via an iPhone Shortcut, plus (stretch) a tiny
watch app that counts how much the weak arm is used during the day .
Honest constraints that shape the plan:
No model training. Pretrained MediaPipe models find body and hand points; our code
does the geometry .
The healthy arm is the target. No medical target numbers needed: progress = weak
arm as a % of healthy arm.
The care team decides. The app suggests; a therapist or doctor approves every plan
change.
Not an emergency device. The app shows stroke warning signs and tells patients to
call 911. It does not detect strokes.
Stroke in two minutes (for every teammate)
A stroke happens when blood stops reaching part of the brain, either because a clot
blocks a vessel or a vessel bursts. Brain cells in that area die within minutes. Each side of
the brain controls the opposite side of the body , so the result is often weakness on one
side, called hemiparesis.

How recovery works: dead cells don't come back, but healthy parts of the brain can learn
to take over (neuroplasticity). That learning needs lots of repetition, ideally every day ,
and most recovery happens in the first months. P atients see a therapist a few times a
week at most; the rest of the time nobody knows whether they're practising, or practising
correctly .
Why arm use matters as much as arm ability: stroke survivors used the affected arm
much less than healthy people used their non-dominant arm, across every type of daily
activity (Sensors 2025). Not using it leads to further decline —
"use it or lose it"
.

Rehab loop · enrollment, 5 repeating steps
Only two steps need a human decision: enrollment (once) and plan approval (step 4).
Everything else runs automatically .
Patient session flow
A session takes about 10–15 minutes and needs one tap to start. Design for someone who
may have only one usable hand, tires easily , or struggles with small text.
1. Home screen — big "Start today's session" button, today's exercises, days until next
check-in, and a red "Stroke warning signs — call 911" link always visible.
2. Quick check-in — two taps: pain today (0–10 slider) and tiredness (emoji scale).
3. Camera setup — on-screen outline shows where to sit; the app waits until it can see
both shoulders, elbows and wrists, then says "Ready" out loud.
4. Exercise, healthy arm first — sets the day's target. V oice counts reps; the angle shows
as a big filling bar .
5. Same exercise, weak arm — the bar shows the healthy arm's mark as the goal. Live
warning if the patient leans or hikes the shoulder:
"Sit back against the chair .
"
6. Repeat for the other exercises (three in total, below).
7. Session summary — one number per exercise ("W eak arm reached 64% of your
healthy arm — up from 58% last week"), plus a streak counter .

Hand opening: average distance from the five fingertips to the wrist, divided by palm size
(wrist to middle-finger knuckle). Dividing by palm size makes it work at any distance from
the camera.
3D replay for the care team: the pose model also outputs an estimated 3D position for
each joint in metres. Save the main 13 joints at 15 frames per second to a file per session.
The dashboard draws them as a stick figure (Three.js) that the therapist can rotate and
scrub through, which makes trunk lean and shoulder hike obvious. Depth is estimated, so
the 3D view is for watching, not for the numbers.
Accuracy rules:
Upper body fully in frame, good front lighting, plain background, short or fitted sleeves.
A verage angles over the last 5 frames; ignore frames where joint confidence is below
0.5.
Biggest risk: stiff , curled stroke hands can confuse the hand model. T est on day 1 with a
teammate holding a loose fist; if detection f ails, show "open your hand toward the
camera as f ar as you can" and record what it can see.

First-hour task: open Shortcuts on a real iPhone and check which of these types appear

Watch caveats:
AFib features. AFib History needs a doctor's AFib diagnosis; irregular rhythm
notifications are for people without one (Apple). P atients will have one or the other .
Blood pressure source. Apple's hypertension notifications aren't meant for people
already diagnosed with hypertension (Apple), which many stroke survivors are. Use
cuff readings logged in the Health app instead.
Why the arm-use stretch goal is worth it:
Established method. Researchers measure real-world arm use after stroke with an
accelerometer on each wrist and compare affected to less-affected side as a use ratio
(review table).
Same idea as ours. A 2026 study paired camera-based reach measurement with a
commercial smartwatch worn on the affected arm (Sensors 2026).
Known flaw. Wrist sensors count walking and whole-body movement as arm use,
overestimating it (JNER 2021). The two-wrist ratio reduces this, because walking
moves both arms (JRM).
Bonus feature: the watch buzzes "use your right arm" after a long idle stretch. In a
2024 home study , affected-arm use measured by the sensors was higher in the group
that got vibration reminders (JNER 2024).
Always prepare Route C: a recorded JSON of three weeks of W atch data per demo
patient, loaded before judging.

Care team: what they see and what they approve
In real stroke rehab, a physical or occupational therapist sets and adjusts exercises, and a
doctor oversees health risks like blood pressure and heart rhythm. Our dashboard serves
both; call them "the care team" in the pitch.
Dashboard screens:
1. Patient list — flagged patients on top, each with symmetry trend arrow , sessions done
this week, and next check-in.
2. Patient detail — symmetry % per exercise over weeks, compensation rate, pain and
tiredness reports, W atch trends (resting heart rate, blood pressure, activity , weak-arm
use).
3. Session replay — 3D stick figure of any session, rotatable, with the angle graph
underneath.
4. Plan editor — the app's suggested next plan, pre-filled; the clinician edits or approves
with one click and sets the next check-in date.
